Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

Bank of Japan board member Naoki Tamura called on Thursday for interest rates to rise to a neutral level "soon". He repeated his view that neutral sits around 2% and warned that the central bank may need to speed up its hikes if inflation pressures build.

Tamura, one of the board's most hawkish members, was speaking to local leaders in Yamanashi at 0100 GMT (2000 US Eastern on Wednesday). His remarks reinforce expectations of a rate rise at the BoJ's December 17-18 meeting and suggest he wants further increases well into 2027.

Policy is still loose, and neutral is a long way off

The BoJ raised its short-term policy rate to around 1.25% in September. Tamura said financial conditions remain accommodative and the policy rate is "still some way" from neutral, the level that neither heats nor cools the economy. He said he first put neutral at around 2% in June and that his view hasn't changed.

That gap matters for markets. If Tamura is right, the BoJ would need roughly three more quarter-point hikes to reach neutral, more than many investors expect. He was candid that neutral can't be known in advance and can only be found by raising rates and watching how the economy responds. The point echoes the RBA's discussion of uncertainty around the neutral rate in its latest Bulletin.

Make haste slowly

Tamura described the right approach as "make haste slowly". He argued that the BoJ can judge the impact of each hike within a few months by talking to firms and reading surveys, and that a thorough assessment doesn't have to be a slow one. Most firms, he said, see the effect of past and near-term hikes on their business as limited, although some have started to warn that higher borrowing costs will restrain investment.

He also flagged the option of moving faster. Inflation expectations in Japan have only recently risen from very low levels, so Tamura said they are less firmly anchored than in the United States or Europe. In his view, that means the BoJ should respond more quickly and strongly to signs that inflation is overshooting, including by speeding up the pace of hikes.

Upside risks are building

Tamura laid out why he is worried about inflation running above the 2% target. Producer prices rose 7.1% from a year earlier in October, driven by high oil prices linked to the Middle East conflict, the weak yen and AI-related demand. Those costs are now reaching consumers. He said CPI inflation excluding temporary government measures has risen to around 3.5%, and pointed to sharp price increases in items such as plastic goods and laptops.

He was also struck by how far the AI boom reaches. He said it is helping a much wider range of Japanese industries than he expected, from data centres to power equipment, air conditioning and construction. He described Japan as rich in "pick-and-shovel" firms, the businesses that supply the tools for a boom rather than taking part in it directly, a reference to the suppliers who profited most from the California Gold Rush. He added that AI is likely to push prices up in the short term, even if it lowers them later through higher productivity.

What it means for the yen

Tamura's views are well known, so the speech is unlikely to shift December pricing much on its own. The more important signal is his push for a faster path toward 2%, which is at the top end of market pricing for an endpoint of around 1.75% to 2%. A more hawkish path would generally support the yen, which is trading near 159 per dollar, close to levels that have previously raised the risk of intervention by Japanese authorities. The weak yen is itself one of the inflation pressures Tamura cites, which makes his case for steady hikes harder to dismiss.

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.
