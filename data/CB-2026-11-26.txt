[2026-11-25 00:48 UTC] Australia trimmed mean CPI jumps to 3.8% in October, keeping further RBA hike risk alive
URL: https://investinglive.com/central-banks/australia-trimmed-mean-cpi-jumps-to-3-8-in-october-keeping-further-rba-hike-risk-alive/
Tags: RBA, AUD, Economics
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

Australia's trimmed mean CPI rose to 3.8% y/y in October from 3.6%, beating forecasts, with headline at 4.9%. A December RBA hold remains likely given rising unemployment, but further hikes cannot be ruled out with core inflation still climbing.

The core beat should support the Australian dollar and push front-end yields higher as traders trim bets on 2027 rate cuts and price in some risk of another hike. December pricing is unlikely to change much, given the weak labour market and the RBA's view that policy is already restrictive. The bigger shift is for the February meeting, which now looks more open. A firmer AUD would ease some imported inflation pressure, while rate-sensitive sectors on the ASX, including property and consumer discretionary, may come under pressure if markets price a longer period of high rates.

Australian core inflation rose further from target in October, leaving a December hold the likeliest outcome but putting further RBA hikes back in play for 2027 and pushing rate-cut hopes further out.

Trimmed mean rose 0.5% m/m, against expectations of 0.3%

Headline CPI rose 4.9% y/y (expected 4.7%, prior 4.3%) and 0.5% m/m (expected 0.4%)

The weighted median held at 3.4% y/y and rose 0.3% m/m

December is still seen as a hold, but more hikes cannot be ruled out, and the first 2027 decision is due on 9 February

Australia's underlying inflation accelerated more than expected in October, with the Reserve Bank of Australia's preferred trimmed mean measure rising to 3.8% from a year earlier, up from 3.6% in September and above the 3.6% forecast, according to figures from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. The trimmed mean climbed 0.5% over the month, outpacing expectations of 0.3% and the 0.3% gain the previous month.

Headline inflation also topped forecasts, rising 0.5% in the month to take the annual rate to 4.9%, up from 4.3% and above the 4.7% expected. Higher fuel prices and the unwinding of electricity subsidies had been flagged as the main drivers of the headline increase, but the strength of the core measures points to broader price pressure. The weighted median, another underlying gauge, held at 3.4% year on year, with a 0.3% monthly rise.

The figures challenge the view, widely held ahead of the release, that October would mark the peak in inflation. The RBA's own November forecasts had headline inflation topping out at 4.5%, and the trimmed mean is now further above the bank's 2% to 3% target range than it was a month ago.

The RBA has raised its cash rate by 125 basis points this year, to 4.85%, as inflation stayed well above target. Its November decision was split, with three of nine board members preferring to hold, and the statement dropped its warning that further increases might be needed. A sharp rise in unemployment to 4.9% in October had since reinforced expectations that the tightening cycle was over.

A hold at the 7 to 8 December meeting still looks the most likely outcome, given the weakening labour market and the RBA's assessment that policy is now clearly restrictive. However, with core inflation moving further from target, further rate increases cannot be ruled out. The board's first decision of 2027 is due on 9 February, with two more monthly inflation reports due before then, and those figures are likely to determine whether the October jump proves a one-off or the start of a more persistent trend. Markets that had priced a first rate cut in the second half of 2027 may need to push that timing back.

Reserve Bank of Australia Governor Bullock facing stagflationary pressures.

Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

RBA's David Jacobs says policy is clearly restrictive after five hikes to 4.85%, biting hardest in housing. But low credit spreads and strong business lending blur the picture, and he says a global urge to invest may be lifting the neutral rate.

Jacobs' remarks support the market view that the RBA's tightening cycle is over, with policy now judged restrictive and housing taking the strain. That leans toward a softer Australian dollar and lower front-end yields over time. His point that a higher neutral rate may be blunting policy is a two-sided risk, though: if inflation proves sticky, it could argue against cutting as early as markets hope. Today's October CPI is the immediate test. A firm trimmed mean would strengthen the "tight on paper, less so in practice" reading and could push back pricing for 2027 cuts.

Main article:Australian monetary policy is now clearly restrictive after five interest rate increases this year, according to David Jacobs, head of domestic markets at the Reserve Bank of Australia, though he cautioned that the degree of tightness is harder to pin down than the level of rates alone would suggest. Speaking at an event in Sydney on Wednesday, Jacobs said the expected real cash rate had moved above estimates of the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, Reuters reported.

Jacobs said the effects were most visible in interest-rate-sensitive parts of the economy, particularly housing. He also flagged a risk that the squeeze could prove stronger than intended, noting that restrictiveness could bite harder if the economic fallout from the housing downturn turns out to be larger than expected.

The picture elsewhere is less clear. Credit spreads remain low and business lending is strong, which Jacobs said makes it difficult to judge how restrictive conditions really are. In his view, the effects of tighter financial conditions are not as even across the economy as the high level of real interest rates might imply.

Part of the explanation, he suggested, may lie outside Australia. A strong global preference to invest rather than save could be lifting the neutral rate, which would reduce the restraint delivered by any given cash rate. If that is the case, a policy setting that looks tight on paper may be exerting less pressure than it appears.

Jacobs stopped short of drawing a policy conclusion, saying that whether the current stance is restrictive enough to bring inflation back to target is a matter for the RBA board.

The RBA has lifted its cash rate to 4.85%, an 18-year high, in its effort to contain inflation. A sharp rise in unemployment, to 4.9% in October, has since led markets to bet that rates have peaked, with a first cut priced for the second half of 2027. The board meets again on 7 and 8 December, when a hold is widely expected. October consumer price data, due later on Wednesday, is the next test, with headline inflation forecast to rise to 4.8%.
