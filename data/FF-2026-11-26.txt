Interest rates remained higher in the UK though, where household consumption growth had been notably more subdued since 2022. I worried this protracted squeeze on living standards risked weighing on growth of supply capacity and eventually increasing the output cost of achieving a given reduction in inflation. The outbreak of severe disruption to energy supply and shipping in the Middle East threatened to derail domestic disinflation in the UK and raised the possibility that a more restrictive policy stance would be necessary to contain renewed price pressures. Against the backdrop of subdued demand and a stagnant labour market, limited evidence of pass-through in the months that followed supported maintaining Bank Rate in restrictive territory. Together with tighter financial conditions, this provided insurance against upside inflation risks while allowing a clearer assessment of the durable scale of the first-round shock before the crucial winter wage- and price-setting period. By November, the available evidence pointed to a large and persistent energy shock, with higher energy bills and fuel prices already pushing up inflation. Growth had been firmer than anticipated, partly supported by AI-related services and investment, although energy and mortgage costs continued to constrain consumption. Labour market conditions appeared to have stabilised, with vacancies at a much lower level than before the pandemic. The outbreak of conflict in the Middle East in late February significantly changed that outlook as disruption to global energy supplies pushed up energy prices and the near-term path for inflation. I voted to hold Bank Rate throughout this period until November when I voted, with most of the Committee, to increase Bank Rate by 25bps to 4%. The considerations underpinning these decisions have evolved. At the end of last year, the key question for me was how quickly restriction could safely be withdrawn as disinflation continued.
