Members began their discussion by noting the increase in market expectations for near-term policy rates in a wide range of advanced economies. Although the scale of the increase had differed somewhat across countries, the pick-up had been underpinned by a range of common factors: resilient economic activity, persistent underlying inflationary pressures and higher oil prices as the Middle East conflict broadened and escalated. Several central banks abroad had already raised their policy rates, and market pricing implied an expectation that monetary policy would be tightened in most major advanced economies over the course of 2026 and 2027. In Australia, market expectations for the cash rate target had also risen since the previous meeting, though by less than in some other countries. This followed stronger-than-expected data on inflation and activity in Australia, higher oil prices and recent RBA communication. Market pricing implied an expectation that the cash rate target would be increased at this meeting, followed by an additional increase of around 15 basis points by the end of the year and another 30 basis points in the first half of 2027. All surveyed market economists expected an increase in the cash rate target at this meeting and several expected a further increase later in the year. Longer term government bond yields had increased notably across advanced economies since the previous meeting. Despite the sharp rises in yields, market functioning had remained orderly. Members observed that the rise in longer term bond yields mainly reflected a rise in real yields, rather than higher long-term inflation expectations (which remained broadly consistent with central bank targets). That was consistent with market expectations that monetary policy would do enough to reduce inflationary pressures. Higher longer term real yields in turn appeared to reflect some combination of an increase in expected long-run neutral rates and term premia.
