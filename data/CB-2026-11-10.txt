Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

It was a pre-emptive hike, and the minutes don’t hide it. Core inflation is near 2%, CPI ex-gasoline is 2.2% and unemployment is stuck around 6.5%. That’s not the profile of an economy that needs tighter policy. The Bank of Canada hiked anyway because Tiff Macklem is a general fighting the last war.

The key line is that the energy shock alone keeps CPI near 3% for more than a year, even with zero pass-through. Add in businesses telling the BOC they plan to pass on more costs and you get a central bank that has stopped looking through oil. Eight months into the war, “transitory” isn’t an argument anyone wants to make.

That said, this isn’t a central bank on autopilot. The case for holding was real, trade talks with the US have broken down and business sentiment has soured. The next move depends on oil and and that has a silver lining as it's fallen fast. However, if core CPI ticks higher in the next two reports, December is live. If it doesn’t, they can afford to wait.

Summary

Philadelphia Fed President Paulson said further gradual rate hikes seem appropriate, with policy needing to be somewhat restrictive. She would act faster if inflation expectations drift up, wage pressures build or underlying inflation moves higher.

Paulson’s comments add a voting member’s voice to the hawkish chorus after the hot CPI report, which reinforces pricing for a December hike and a follow-up in January. Her emphasis on gradual steps tempers the case for a half-point move, but her three triggers for faster action leave that door open if data keeps running hot. That combination supports the US dollar and front-end Treasury yields, while a firmer hiking path remains a headwind for gold. Because she names energy prices as an inflation driver, any further Middle East-driven gains in oil now feed directly into the Fed’s tightening calculus.

Paulson favours steady hikes rather than sprints, but has set out exactly what would make her pick up the pace.

Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson, a 2026 FOMC voter, said further gradual tightening seems appropriate

She said policy needs to be somewhat restrictive to return inflation to 2%, with underlying inflation stuck between 2.5% and 3%

She would favour moving faster if inflation expectations drift up, wage pressures build or underlying inflation rises

Paulson said the October CPI rise of 0.4% shows inflation pressures persist, driven by tariffs, energy and AI investment

Futures price strong odds of a quarter-point hike on December 8-9 from the current 3.75% to 4% range, and another in January

Philadelphia Federal Reserve President Anna Paulson said on Tuesday that further gradual interest rate increases appear appropriate to bring down elevated inflation, and that she would favour moving faster if price pressures worsen.

In the text of a speech prepared for a gathering in Conshohocken, Pennsylvania, Paulson said monetary policy needs to be somewhat restrictive to return inflation to the Fed’s 2% target. Inflation has run above that goal for more than five years, she noted, with underlying pressures holding between 2.5% and 3%. She said the exact path of rates will depend on how conditions evolve and that she will be watching the data closely.

Paulson, a voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee this year, argued that a gradual approach has served the Fed well. In her view, it gives the economy time to adjust to higher borrowing costs while allowing policymakers room to assess the effects of their decisions. She said she has supported the Fed’s recent rate decisions and described bringing inflation down as her main priority, with the economy proving stronger than expected and the job market robust and stable.

She also set out the conditions under which she would favour a quicker pace. Paulson said she would want to act more quickly if inflation expectations drift higher, if wage pressures build, or if underlying inflation rises. She described risks to the inflation outlook as high and said the effects of AI and energy on prices are hard to gauge.

Her remarks came after October consumer price data showed a larger-than-expected rise, with the overall index up 0.4% on the month. Paulson said the report suggests inflation pressures remain very much present, pointing to tariffs, energy prices and AI investment as drivers. Economists saw signs that price increases once concentrated in a few areas are spreading more broadly.

Futures markets are pricing strong odds of a quarter-point rate hike at the Fed’s December 8-9 meeting, from the current target range of 3.75% to 4%, and a further increase in late January. Before the CPI release, Governor Christopher Waller said a reading like October’s could lead him to support a half-point move. The December meeting is now the key test of whether the Fed sticks with gradual steps or speeds up.

[2026-11-10 21:42 UTC] More from Fed’s Bowman: Shifts focus to inflation, wants AI and Middle East risks to ease first
URL: https://investinglive.com/central-banks/more-from-fed-s-bowman-shifts-focus-to-inflation-wants-ai-and-middle-east-risks-to-ease-first/
Tags: Fed
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman said she is shifting her focus to inflation, which is not on a downward trend, and that AI and Middle East risks must ease before she is confident it falls next year. Expectations remain well anchored, she said.

Overall the comments lean hawkish. Bowman’s shift in focus to inflation and her link to Middle East risk keep energy prices at the centre of the Fed debate, which supports the US dollar and front-end Treasury yields. Her emphasis on pace and timeline suggests a preference for gradual tightening rather than aggressive moves, which may temper any repricing toward bigger or faster hikes. For gold, a firmer case for further tightening has been a headwind during this conflict, not a support. Oil traders may read her remarks as a reminder that any further Middle East escalation now feeds directly into Fed policy risk.

She said the Fed needs to consider the pace and timeline of its response, citing last year’s gradual cuts, pause and resumption as a model

On supervision, she expects to finalise the GSIB surcharge and Basel III rules in the coming weeks, with her focus moving to smaller-bank rules

Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman said she is shifting her focus to inflation, arguing that the lack of a downward trend in price pressures should give policymakers pause. She added that she would need to see risks tied to artificial intelligence and the Middle East fade before she could be confident inflation will come down next year.

The remarks, made in a question-and-answer session on Tuesday, followed earlier comments in which Bowman said persistent inflation and firming labour market conditions suggest the Fed still has work to do. In those remarks, she estimated that core PCE inflation likely ran at 3.3% in October, driven by energy prices and AI-related demand.

Her latest comments were more mixed in tone. On one side, Bowman said longer-term inflation expectations remain very well anchored. She called expectations an important data point but said they do not necessarily drive her thinking, which suggests anchored expectations alone will not settle her policy view.

On the other side, she said the Fed needs to think about the pace and timeline for addressing inflation. She drew a parallel with the central bank’s approach last year, when it cut rates gradually, paused, and then resumed. In her view, a similarly measured, step-by-step approach could be used to tackle inflation in the coming months. That framing points to a preference for gradual tightening over a rapid move, rather than signalling any specific decision.

On the labour market, Bowman said she does not expect AI to rob the US economy of jobs. She expects the technology to make workers more productive, create new roles and remove less necessary ones.

Bowman also covered her supervisory brief. She said she hopes and expects to finalise rules on the surcharge for global systemically important banks (GSIBs) and on Basel III capital standards in the coming weeks, and that her attention has turned to rules and supervisory approaches for smaller banks. She noted that the Fed carries out ongoing cybersecurity examinations of banks and has seen a strong focus on finding and patching vulnerabilities. She also said open lines of communication with the Treasury are critically important.

Taken together, the comments keep the inflation fight at the centre of Bowman’s thinking, while her emphasis on pace suggests she favours a measured path. Upcoming inflation data, and any easing in energy and AI-related price pressures, will be key to how far that view moves.
