Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

Japan spent a record $50 billion in a single day to support the yen in late July, official data released on Monday showed. The figures confirm the scale of an intervention campaign that markets had long suspected and that included rare coordinated action with the United States.

Bessent’s comments also fed speculation about faster tightening by the Bank of Japan. For traders, the message is that Tokyo has shown it is willing to spend heavily, and alongside Washington, to defend the yen. The currency’s direction, though, still depends largely on the gap between US and Japanese interest rates.

Summary

BoJ Summary of Opinions shows members agree inflation is at target, with several urging a faster move to neutral, which one sees considerably above 1%. Others flag weak consumption and lagged hike effects. Markets eye a December hike to 1.5%.

The Summary should firm pricing for a December hike to 1.5% and support the yen against the US dollar. It could also lift short-dated Japanese government bond yields, since the debate is now about pace, not direction. The member putting neutral considerably above 1% matters for terminal-rate pricing: it suggests markets could push their end-point expectations higher than the roughly 2% some already pencil in for next year. The BoJ’s argument is no longer about whether to keep hiking but how fast, and the hawks are making the louder case.

The BoJ board judged underlying inflation to be generally consistent with its 2% target, shifting the debate to the pace of hikes

One member called for stepping up the pace to neutral, another urged reaching it soon but to “make haste slowly”, and a third backed getting there early

One member said the neutral rate is likely considerably above 1%; core CPI is projected to exceed 3% toward the end of the fiscal year

More cautious members cited the lagged effects of past hikes, and one warned the policy rate is already within the estimated neutral range

Members flagged weak consumption momentum and uncertainty over how long AI-related demand will last; they also backed faster ETF sales

The Summary supports expectations for a hike to 1.5% at the December 17-18 meeting

Bank of Japan policymakers broadly agreed at their October meeting that the next move is a further rate hike, according to the Summary of Opinions released on Tuesday. Several pushed to reach the neutral rate soon, and one argued that neutral is likely “considerably above 1 percent.”

The summary, an anonymous digest of views from the October 29 and 30 meeting, shows the board judged underlying inflation to be generally consistent with the 2% price target. With that point settled, the debate has moved from whether to hike to how quickly. One member described a “new phase” in which the bank needs to step up the pace of bringing rates to neutral, noting that the effects of past hikes have yet to materialise and financial conditions remain accommodative. Another said the bank should reach neutral soon but “make haste slowly,” checking financial conditions for a few months after each move. A third called for a regime shift to get rates to neutral at an early stage, giving the bank room to respond nimbly in either direction.

Inflation risks featured prominently. Members noted that core CPI is projected to exceed 3% toward the end of the fiscal year and that inflation has picked up with the rise in crude oil prices. They also said Japan’s inflation expectations are less firmly anchored than those in Europe or the US, leaving the economy more exposed to external shocks.

The more cautious voices were also on record. Some members favoured holding in October to examine risks and the cumulative effect of past hikes. One said underlying inflation can be anchored around 2% without hastily raising rates to neutral. Another warned that with the policy rate already within the estimated neutral range, a further hike could weigh on the economy.

Consumption was a recurring concern. One member said private consumption lacks momentum and is unlikely to lead the economy. Another said the bank should not be overly cautious about the next hike but should assess the underlying trend in spending. That point gained weight on Tuesday, when data showed September household spending fell 6.8% from a year earlier, far worse than expected.

Members also flagged uncertainty over how long AI-related demand will support Japan’s economy, and backed the decision to speed up the bank’s ETF sales. Government representatives said they expect the bank to cooperate closely with the government. Taken together, the summary supports market expectations for a hike to 1.5% at the December 17-18 meeting, with consumption and next year’s wage talks the main checks on the pace.

Bank of Japan: Summary of Opinions (October 29-30 meeting)

The Bank of Japan releases the Summary of Opinions from its October 29 and 30 policy meeting at 23:50 GMT on Monday (18:50 US Eastern time).

The Summary is a short, anonymous digest of the views board members and government representatives gave at the meeting. It comes out about a week and a half after the decision. Speakers are not named, and the governor, as chair, edits the document. Its value is speed: it gives an early read on where the debate stands, how many members lean toward the next move, and what conditions they want met first. The minutes are a different product. They come out only after the following meeting, so the October minutes are due on December 23, after the December decision. They give a fuller account of the discussion, but by then they are largely history. The Summary is the one that can move the yen.

At the meetingthe BoJ held its policy rate at 1.25%, a 31-year high, on an 8-1 vote. Board member Hajime Takata dissented and proposed a hike to 1.5%, on the view that underlying inflation has reached the 2% target. The Bank said underlying inflation is now generally consistent with its target and that risks to prices are skewed to the upside. It raised its fiscal 2027 core inflation forecast to 2.6% from 2.4% and trimmed growth to 0.7%. It also surprised markets by more than doubling the pace of its ETF sales to around 860 billion yen a year from January 2027. Governor Kazuo Ueda then softened the inflation message at his press conference.

The focus is December. The BoJ raised rates in June and September, and markets widely expect it to keep that quarterly pace with a hike to 1.5% at the December 17-18 meeting. Watch how many members say conditions for the next step are in place, whether anyone else shares Takata’s sense of urgency, and whether the more cautious members point to the softer growth outlook or to oil-driven import costs. A Summary heavy with near-term hike support would firm December pricing and help the yen. A guarded tone would leave the timing open.

China: October trade data

China publishes October trade figures on Tuesday. A Reuters poll expects exports to have grown about 33% year on year in dollar terms, slightly slower than September’s 36% surge, with imports seen up around 32%. The surplus is expected near $126 billion, down from about $134 billion in September but enough to take the total for the first ten months of 2026 above $1 trillion. Demand for AI infrastructure hardware has driven the export boom, though some analysts read October’s fall in new export orders as an early warning that the tech cycle is softening.

The equity backdrop matters more. The CSI 300 fell 8.5% in October, its worst month in over a decade, and posted a record 12 straight weekly losses on a weak economy and doubts about AI earnings. Beijing first called on state funds, the so-called “national team,” to buy shares and on companies to step up buybacks. On November 2 the PBOC and the securities regulator brought back the 2024 toolkit. They are expanding the swap facility that funds stock purchases by brokers, funds and insurers. They cut the relending rate for buybacks to 1% from 1.25% and made that facility permanent. They also eased the rules on buybacks. Investors were sceptical at first, and the state funds then bought more. So the Bank of Japan is speeding up its ETF sales while Beijing’s state funds are buying. A strong trade print may help sentiment at the margin, but analysts argue a lasting turnaround needs household-focused fiscal support and a steadier property market.
