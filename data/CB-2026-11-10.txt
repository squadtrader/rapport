Markets price three more BoE hikes after last week’s unanimous rise to 4%, but many analysts see just one more, likely in February, calling it an insurance move against energy-driven inflation rather than the start of an aggressive cycle.

The gap between futures pricing and analyst expectations is the key tension for UK assets. Markets are pricing three more Bank of England rate hikes by the end of next year, but analysts reacting to last week’s move increasingly doubt the central bank will need to go that far. Most see one further increase as the more likely path.

The BoE raised Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4% in a unanimous decision, aiming to contain inflation that is running at 3.5% and still rising. The move was widely expected and caused little disruption in markets, but analysts said the unanimity could reinforce expectations of further tightening.

The driver is the energy shock from the war with Iran. Policymakers are concerned that higher energy costs will pass through to consumers and become embedded in the economy, and they are using higher borrowing costs to cool demand. One forecast sees inflation peaking later and higher than previously thought, at around 4.5% next summer, as energy prices continue to grind higher. The key question, analysts said, is whether this imported shock stays contained or spills into domestic wages and services prices.

Several analysts characterised the decision as a risk-management or insurance hike rather than the start of an aggressive cycle. In this view, the Bank is acting to protect its credibility and limit second-round effects, even though evidence of energy costs becoming embedded in wider wages and prices remains limited. Resilient growth, consumer spending and labour market data have given it both a reason to hike and the room to do so.

That has opened a gap between market pricing and economists’ expectations. Futures imply significantly more tightening than most forecasters anticipate. Higher bond yields are already doing some of the Bank’s work, and higher energy bills, mortgage rates and tighter financial conditions are expected to weigh on household spending. One analyst said the move could prove “one and done” if energy prices ease before February, while others see one further hike, most likely in February, as the base case.

The outlook remains closely tied to the Middle East. A prolonged conflict that keeps oil prices elevated into 2027 would strengthen the case for further tightening. Analysts flagged a return to US strikes on Iran, or rising domestic political uncertainty, as risks to a shallower path.

For investors, a number of analysts see value in gilts after the rise in yields. Some favour the seven- to eight-year part of the curve and gradually extending duration, arguing that much of the UK’s fiscal and supply risk is already priced. For sterling, the key issue is whether the BoE ends up tightening much more than other major central banks, which analysts view as unlikely for now.

[2026-11-09 00:25 UTC] PBOC is expected to set the USD/CNY reference rate at 6.6799 – Reuters estimate
URL: https://investinglive.com/central-banks/pboc-is-expected-to-set-the-usd-cny-reference-rate-at-6-6799-reuters-estimate/
Tags: CNY, CNH, pboc
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

Summary

Japan spent a record $50 billion on July 30 to prop up the yen, data showed, part of about $97 billion across July and August that included rare joint action with the US. The yen still trades near 158 per dollar as US-Japan rate gaps persist.

The data confirms Tokyo will spend heavily, and alongside Washington, to defend the yen. That keeps intervention risk firmly in play as USD/JPY trades near 158, well within reach of this year’s low near 164. It caps the upside for dollar-yen and makes long positions more two-way, including Morgan Stanley’s carry-driven long USD/JPY call. Japan spent a record $50 billion in a single day to support the yen in late July, official data released on Monday showed. The figures confirm the scale of an intervention campaign that markets had long suspected and that included rare coordinated action with the United States.

Ministry of Finance figures showed Tokyo bought a total of around 15.4 trillion yen, roughly $97 billion, across July and August, theWall Street Journal (gated)reported. Almost 8 trillion yen of that was deployed on July 30 alone, the largest one-day intervention on record. The total for the two months exceeded the amount spent in April and May.

The figures are not a surprise in themselves, since the operations were widely flagged at the time, but they put hard numbers on how forcefully Tokyo moved. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama and US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confirmed in late July that the two countries had intervened jointly to support the yen, the first coordinated operation of its kind since 1998.

The scale reflects mounting alarm in Tokyo after the yen slid to a four-decade low near 164 per dollar earlier this year. Bessent was outspoken about the currency’s decline over the summer. He backed Japan’s efforts to counter what he described as its undervaluation and pointed to the role a weak yen was playing in pushing up domestic inflation.

Yet the data also highlight the limits of intervention. The yen initially strengthened after the joint action, but it is still trading around 158 per dollar. Rising expectations of further Federal Reserve rate hikes have reinforced the view that the wide interest rate gap between the US and Japan will persist, keeping pressure on the currency.

Bessent’s comments also fed speculation about faster tightening by the Bank of Japan. The BOJ raised rates to 1.25% in September and is expected to move again before the end of the year, having cited yen weakness as a key factor shaping its economic and inflation outlook. Governor Kazuo Ueda said on Friday that he sees some upside risks to inflation as energy costs feed through to consumer prices.

For traders, the message is that Tokyo has shown it is willing to spend heavily, and alongside Washington, to defend the yen. The currency’s direction, though, still depends largely on the gap between US and Japanese interest rates.
