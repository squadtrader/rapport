Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

China’s central bank will require banks to hold a larger share of their foreign currency deposits in reserve. The move is a technical tightening of onshore dollar liquidity that markets are likely to read as a signal that Beijing wants to slow the yuan’s rise.

The People’s Bank of China said it will raise the foreign exchange deposit reserve ratio for financial institutions by 2 percentage points, to 6% from 4%, from November 15. It described the step as a way to strengthen the management of foreign exchange liquidity in the banking system.

The ratio works much like the better-known reserve requirement on yuan deposits, but it applies to deposits held in foreign currencies, mostly US dollars. When the ratio rises, banks must park more of those dollars with the central bank, leaving fewer available to lend, invest or sell in the onshore market. With dollars harder to come by, they become relatively more expensive against the yuan, which takes some pressure off the Chinese currency’s appreciation.

The PBOC uses the tool in both directions. It raised the ratio twice in 2021, when the yuan was climbing strongly, and cut it in 2022 and again in 2023 as the currency weakened and the central bank sought to support it. The ratio had stood at 4% since the last cut, making this the first increase in several years.

The size of the move is modest. When the PBOC cut the ratio by the same 2 percentage points in 2022, ANZ estimated it would add around $20 billion of foreign currency to the market, which is small relative to China’s currency market. The signal, rather than the cash amount, is the main point.

Other indicators point the same way. On Monday, the PBOC set its daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 6.71, considerably weaker for the yuan than the roughly 6.66 that Reuters’ survey of models had expected. That suggests the central bank was already leaning against the currency’s strength. The PBOC has also said it aims to keep the yuan broadly stable and to guard against the risk of the exchange rate overshooting.

For traders, the practical takeaway is that Beijing is signalling a preference for slower, steadier yuan gains. It is not reversing the currency’s direction. The daily fixing will be the clearest guide to whether more steps follow, particularly if the US dollar weakens further.

The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.85% on Tuesday, its fifth increase this year and the highest level since 2008. A three-member dissent, however, signalled growing division over how much further policy needs to tighten.

Six members of the Monetary Policy Board voted for the hike, while three preferred to keep the rate at 4.60%. In its statement, the board said inflation remains elevated and broadly based, and that there is an increasing risk higher inflation expectations will become embedded. It judged that further tightening in financial conditions was warranted to support the return of inflation to target.

The accompanying Statement on Monetary Policy showed a material upgrade to the inflation outlook. Headline inflation is now forecast to peak at 4.5% in the December quarter, and both headline and trimmed mean inflation are expected to stay above 3% until late 2027, about three quarters longer than forecast in August. Inflation is not expected to return to the 2.5% midpoint of the target band until mid-2028. Headline inflation was 3.9% over the year to the September quarter, with trimmed mean at 3.7%.

The RBA attributed the revision to two factors. Fuel and other commodity prices have risen sharply since the Middle East conflict re-escalated, with Brent crude around US$115 a barrel at the time of the forecasts. Staff also now judge domestic capacity pressures to be tighter than previously thought, and lifted their estimate of the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation to around 4.75%. The bank also noted that AI-related demand is pushing up prices for technology goods, and that some measures of inflation expectations have risen.

The economy is slowing under the weight of higher rates. House prices were almost 5% below their March peak in September, unemployment averaged 4.6% in the September quarter, and the jobless rate is forecast to rise to 5.1% by end-2028. Strong investment in data centres is partly offsetting weaker household spending and housing activity.

The forecasts assume the cash rate rises by around 35 basis points by mid-2027 in line with market pricing, a path up to 50 basis points higher than in August. On guidance, though, the board said only that it will be attentive to the data and will do what is necessary to bring inflation back to target. That stops short of an explicit commitment to further hikes.

The outcome matched expectations from HSBC and AMP, while the split vote and cautious guidance sit closer to AMP’s hike-and-hold view than to ANZ’s warning of a move to 5.10%.
