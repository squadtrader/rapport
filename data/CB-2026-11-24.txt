Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

Summary

New York Fed President John Williams says inflation is still too high with risks skewed higher, backing tighter policy as markets price a quarter-point December hike. He sees inflation ending 2026 at 3.5% and only returning to the 2% goal in 2028.

Williams' remarks support pricing for a quarter-point hike in December and further tightening in 2027, which should keep front-end Treasury yields firm and give the US dollar some support. Energy features in his inflation story, and he calls the energy outlook uncertain, so any renewed rise in oil would add to the hawkish case. For equities, his message suggests the Fed will not step back from tightening just because stocks have been volatile, though the AI-led rally shows risk appetite can hold up under a hiking path. If December data come in hot, traders may start pricing a faster pace of increases next year.

Williams sees growth as strong and the labour market as steady, and with inflation not back at target until 2028, he has no reason to take the Fed off its tightening path.

New York Federal Reserve President John Williams said on Monday that inflation remains too high and the US central bank still has work to do to bring it back to its 2% goal, leaving the door open to another rate increase when policymakers meet on December 8 and 9.

In remarks prepared for an event at the University of Mount Saint Vincent in New York City, Williams said the Fed needs to act over time to return inflation to target in a timely way and keep it there. He added that he will keep weighing incoming data before deciding what the Fed should do at the December meeting, stopping short of committing to a move.

His forecasts underline how long the job could take. Williams expects inflation to finish 2026 at 3.5%, slow to just above 2% next year and reach the target only in 2028. He described the risks to that path as tilted to the upside, pointing to energy prices as one of the main drivers of price pressure and saying their outlook is uncertain.

Williams did offer some reassurance on the breadth of the problem. In his view, tariffs, geopolitical conflicts and the surge in artificial intelligence investment are lifting prices in certain categories of goods, but there is little sign these effects are feeding through into wider, more persistent inflation. He also said the impact of AI on the economy overall remains uncertain.

On growth and jobs, his assessment was upbeat. Williams sees the economy as strong and resilient, forecasting growth of 2.75% in both 2026 and 2027, and described the labour market as stable. He expects the unemployment rate, currently 4.1%, to edge down to around 4% next year.

Markets broadly expect the Fed to lift its policy rate by a quarter of a percentage point in December from the current 3.75% to 4.00% range, with further increases anticipated next year. The Fed last raised rates in September, and inflation has now run above target for more than five years, raising concern among officials that the public could lose faith in a return to 2%.

The remarks landed on a day of sharp gains for stocks, after Nvidia said it was reinstating Jensen Huang as chief executive following a push from President Donald Trump. The rally highlighted how heavily markets lean on AI companies, and came after some observers had questioned whether recent equity losses might make the Fed hesitate. Williams' message suggests stock market swings are unlikely to knock the Fed off its focus on inflation.
