Australia’s unemployment rate jumped to 4.9% in October, above the 4.7% expected, as employment fell 43,500 versus a forecast 25,000 gain. A second month of job losses takes further RBA hikes off the table, and the Aussie fell below 0.6700.

The catch is inflation above 4%, kept elevated by energy costs. That leaves the RBA unable to cut, a stagflation-tinged mix that gives the Aussie little support from either side.

Australia’s unemployment rate jumped to 4.9% in October from 4.7%, well above the 4.7% economists had expected, as employment fell for a second straight month, data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics showed on Thursday. Employment dropped by 43,500, against forecasts for a gain of 25,000, after a 39,300 fall in September. The jobless rate is now the highest since late 2021.

The detail offered little comfort. Economists had expected September’s slump in part-time work to reverse, but part-time employment fell by a further 40,600, while full-time jobs slipped by 2,900 after rising in September. Taken together, the economy has shed more than 80,000 jobs in two months. Unemployment rose even though the participation rate fell to 66.6% from 66.8%. That combination is unusually weak: fewer people were looking for work, yet the share of those who could not find it still climbed.

The result lands squarely in the middle of the Reserve Bank of Australia’s internal debate. Minutes released on Tuesday showed three of nine board members voted against November’s hike to 4.85%, warning the economy might be close to a materially sharper downturn and pointing to a larger-than-expected rise in unemployment. Thursday’s data give that minority fresh ammunition. The RBA’s own forecasts had unemployment reaching 4.9% only by the end of this year, so the labour market has deteriorated faster than the central bank assumed.

The problem for the RBA is that inflation has not gone away. Headline inflation is running above 4%, and data on Wednesday showed annual wage growth of 3.5% in the September quarter, with the quarterly rise the largest since late 2023. The majority on the board judged rates were not yet clearly high enough to contain upside inflation risks, and that concern will not disappear simply because of a weak run of jobs data.

Even so, two consecutive monthly declines in employment should take further rate hikes off the table. A December move was already largely priced out before the release, and it is now hard to see the board tightening into a labour market that is shedding jobs this quickly. February, which some economists had flagged as the next realistic window for a hike, looks to have closed as well. The question for the RBA has shifted from how much further rates need to rise to how long they stay at 4.85%, and with unemployment rising faster than forecast, the conversation could turn to when cuts come onto the agenda if the weakness persists.

The Australian dollar fell on the data, slipping below 0.6700 and below the 10-month low it touched earlier in the week.

Summary

BOJ Governor Ueda said the bank expects to keep raising rates as monetary conditions stay accommodative, warning underlying inflation could overshoot the 2% target. He called consumption resilient, backing bets on a December hike to 1.50%.

Ueda’s remarks firm up the case for a December hike, which should give some support to the yen, although the effect may be limited while the Fed is also raising rates. The emphasis on overshoot risk could matter more for the path beyond December, supporting bets on a move to 1.75% by March and keeping upward pressure on short-dated JGB yields. Japanese bank stocks tend to benefit from a steeper rate path, while exporters lose some of the support from a weak yen. Overnight inflation data will be the next catalyst. A firm print would strengthen expectations for both the December move and the one after it.

Ueda’s worry is no longer inflation falling short of 2%. It’s inflation overshooting, and with policy still accommodative, that points to more hikes, starting in December.

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda said on Thursday that the central bank expects to keep raising interest rates because monetary conditions remain accommodative, adding that there is a risk underlying inflation could overshoot the BOJ’s 2% target. His remarks, which crossed the wires from 01:03 GMT, reinforce expectations of a December hike and come a day after he said the bank should continue lifting borrowing costs.

Ueda described the economy as recovering moderately, albeit with some weakness, and said consumption has been resilient. He judged underlying inflation to be roughly consistent with the price target, but stressed that the bigger danger now lies on the upside. Anchoring underlying inflation around 2%, he said, is important to stop the risk of an overshoot from materialising.

That framing matters. For years the BOJ’s challenge was getting inflation up to target. Ueda is now signalling that the task has flipped to keeping it from running too far above it, and that with real interest rates still negative, policy is far from restrictive. In that light, further hikes look less like tightening for its own sake and more like the gradual removal of stimulus that the economy no longer needs.

The comments sit comfortably with market expectations. In a Reuters poll published on Wednesday, 86% of economists forecast a 25 basis point hike to 1.50% at the December 17 to 18 meeting, which would be the BOJ’s third increase this year, and nearly 60% expect another move to 1.75% by the end of March. Ueda’s description of consumption as resilient also offers some cover for pressing ahead despite the economy’s unexpected contraction in the July to September quarter.

The weak yen remains a key part of the backdrop. The currency is trading near 160 per dollar despite heavy intervention by Japanese authorities this year, and a widening rate gap with a Federal Reserve that is itself still hiking keeps pressure on the BOJ to act. A weak yen raises import costs and adds to the very inflation overshoot risk Ueda highlighted.

The next test comes quickly. Japan’s national consumer price data for October are due at 23:30 GMT on Thursday, and a firm reading would further strengthen the case for a December move.

[2026-11-19 00:28 UTC] Four Fed officials push for more hikes as Waller floats 50bp to break 3% inflation mindset

Summary

Four Fed officials struck hawkish notes on Wednesday. Waller backed a 50bp December hike to break a 3% inflation mindset, Kashkari said markets see more hikes than officials do, and Daly and Barr said inflation has run too high for too long.

The combined message supports the dollar and keeps front-end Treasury yields firm, though markets aren’t chasing Waller’s call for a bigger move. That leaves room for a repricing if December data run hot. If Waller’s communication-first approach wins out, the risk is a more hawkish statement and press conference even with a 25bp hike, which could push expectations for the rate path higher. Gold faces a headwind from rising hike expectations, as it has throughout this cycle. Rate-sensitive AI and growth stocks remain exposed after a bruising week. Elevated oil prices are part of the inflation problem the hawks describe, so energy remains the swing factor for how far they push.

---Wednesday’s Fed speakers agreed that inflation needs more work. The only real disagreement is pace: Waller wants a bigger hike or much tougher guidance, while the rest appear content with steady quarter-point steps.

Governor Waller favours a 50bp hike in December or much tougher forward guidance to break the public’s acceptance of 3% inflation

Waller noted that about 70% of core services components are running above 3% and said the Fed may need to go beyond reversing last year’s cuts

Four Federal Reserve policymakers lined up on Wednesday to argue that the fight against inflation is not finished, with Governor Christopher Waller going furthest by making the case for a 50 basis point hike in December. Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari, San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly and Governor Michael Barr all leaned the same way, pointing to inflation that has stayed too high for too long against a backdrop of resilient spending and a stable jobs market. Together, their remarks reinforce expectations that the Fed will raise rates again next month, with the debate now centred on how hard to push rather than whether to move.

Waller set out the most aggressive view. He is worried that price pressures have spread well beyond energy, noting that roughly 70% of the components in the core services basket are running above 3%. He sees businesses growing comfortable passing higher costs on to customers, partly because they believe consumers can absorb them, and fears the public is starting to accept inflation around 3% as normal after years above the Fed’s 2% target.

To break that psychology, Waller believes the Fed needs to act more forcefully, either by raising rates by 50 basis points in December or by signalling a markedly tougher stance through statements, press conferences and forward guidance. He suggested the Fed may need to go beyond simply reversing the rate cuts it made last year, and questioned how useful the December economic projections will be, preferring sharper forward-looking communication. Waller is, for now, alone among governors in pushing for a bigger move, but his views have often been an early signal of where policy goes next.

Kashkari’s most striking comment was that markets believe the Fed will need to raise rates further than officials currently expect, and he made no effort to talk that pricing down. He traced elevated inflation to a series of supply shocks and said keeping inflation expectations anchored is essential. He described the strength of demand as somewhat surprising and the job market as resilient, and said higher rates can clearly weigh on investment.

Daly described the labour market as stable but lacking dynamism, with employment broad-based and helped by a revival in manufacturing. She said consumer spending remains resilient, even among lower-income households, and that inflation has been too high for too long, while acknowledging uncertainty over how it will evolve. Last week she said further hikes were likely needed, citing inflation spreading into services, though she also noted early signs of consumer fatigue and plans to watch holiday spending closely.

Barr said he would support further rate increases if the economy evolves as he expects, calling inflation too high and a strain on many households. He described the economy as strong with low unemployment and said returning inflation to 2% in a timely way is critical, though he declined to commit to a timetable.

What markets are pricing

Markets were pricing around 61 basis points of hikes over the next three meetings, consistent with a quarter point move in December and more to follow. A 50 basis point step in December, however, was given only about a 5% chance, suggesting traders see Waller’s call as a warning shot rather than the base case. Minutes of the Fed’s last meeting, released on Wednesday, were reported to show officials expect further hikes and are concerned that AI investment could add to inflation, which fits the tone set by all four speakers.

The collective noun for hawks seems to be a "Fed" right now.

Summary

BOJ’s Ueda said real rates remain low and financial conditions loose despite past hikes, with strong profits and rising wages helping firms absorb higher costs. He said the Q3 consumption dip was expected and BOJ finances can cope with tightening.

These follow-up comments matter more for the path beyond December than for the December decision, which already looked well set. By saying real rates are still low and conditions loose, Ueda leaves room for the 1.75% end-March level many economists expect, and possibly more. That supports higher short-dated JGB yields and gives the yen modest support. The reassurance on BOJ finances and ETF sales removes a technical worry that some had seen as a brake on tightening. Japanese equities may watch the ETF comment closely, since the pace of BOJ selling affects a large holder of the market.

Ueda used his follow-up remarks to close off the obvious arguments for waiting. Weak spending was expected, firms can handle higher costs, and the BOJ’s own finances aren’t a constraint, all of which leaves the path clear for more hikes.

Ueda said real interest rates remain low at the short end despite past hikes, and that financial conditions are still loose

He said higher funding costs for firms must be weighed against strong earnings and rising wages

He said the drop in July to September consumption was within BOJ expectations, and that consumption is resilient overall as wages rise

The BOJ will scrutinise how hikes affect corporate and household activity

Ueda said BOJ simulations suggest its policy, including accelerated ETF sales, need not severely damage its finances

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda added detail to his case for further rate hikes on Thursday, arguing that real interest rates remain low despite past increases and that strong corporate earnings and rising wages should help the economy absorb higher borrowing costs. His latest comments, which crossed the wires between 01:17 and 01:35 GMT, followed earlier remarks in which he said the BOJ expects to keep raising rates and warned that underlying inflation could overshoot its 2% target.

Ueda acknowledged that rate hikes push up funding costs for companies, but said any assessment of their impact must also weigh healthy profits and steadily rising pay. Even after the BOJ’s earlier moves, he said, real interest rates remain low at the shorter end of the curve, the part that matters most for economic activity, and overall financial conditions are still loose and supportive. The bank will closely examine how higher rates affect corporate and household behaviour, he added.

He also sought to defuse concern over the weak spot in the latest growth figures. The drop in consumption in the July to September quarter, as shown in the GDP data, was within the BOJ’s expectations, Ueda said, describing consumption as resilient overall as wages climb steadily in a tight labour market. That matters because the unexpected contraction in third-quarter GDP had raised questions about whether the BOJ would pause.

On the central bank’s own balance sheet, Ueda said internal simulations suggest the BOJ can avoid a situation in which its policy, including accelerated sales of its exchange-traded fund holdings, inflicts severe damage on its finances. The issue has drawn attention because higher rates increase the interest the BOJ pays on banks’ reserves while much of its bond portfolio still carries low yields, squeezing its income. Ueda’s reassurance signals that balance sheet worries are not a constraint on further tightening.

Taken together, the comments strip away several possible reasons for the BOJ to hold back in December: weak consumption, corporate funding costs and the bank’s own finances. With real rates still low and financial conditions loose by Ueda’s own assessment, the bar to keep hiking appears low. Markets will look to Japan’s national CPI data at 23:30 GMT on Thursday for further confirmation ahead of the December 17 to 18 meeting.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.
