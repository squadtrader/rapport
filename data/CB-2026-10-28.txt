Moody’s Analytics chief economist Mark Zandi has warned that raising interest rates at this week’s Federal Reserve meeting would be a mistake, arguing that the bond market is already doing the tightening for the central bank and that a policy error now ranks among the growing risks facing the US economy.

Writing on X on Monday, Zandi pointed to the sharp rise in borrowing costs since the Fed’s September hike. The 10-year Treasury yield is hovering around 5%, fixed mortgage rates are above 7% and corporate bond spreads have widened. In his view, that rise in market rates is already restraining the economy, which leaves no need for the Fed to add to it. The FOMC meets on October 27 and 28, with the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00% after September’s quarter-point increase, and markets pricing at least one more hike before year-end.

Oil sits at the centre of his argument. With prices climbing back toward their wartime peaks on tensions with Iran, Zandi said higher energy costs will push up inflation, but warned that rate hikes cannot fix a supply problem. Instead, he argued, higher oil prices will erode real wages and consumer spending, and tightening into that squeeze risks tipping the economy into trouble.

Zandi also pointed to Moody’s risk matrix, which ranks threats to the economy over the next 12 to 18 months by likelihood and potential damage. He said it contains a lot of red, with risks including an AI stock sell-off, a Fed misstep, intensifying wars, trade escalation and financial repression.

The warning is consistent with his earlier stance. Zandi described September’s hike as a serious mistake and warned in late September that higher rates were already damaging the economy. The Fed’s case for tightening rests on inflation that remains well above target, with headline PCE estimated at 3.8% in August and core at 3.4%, alongside price pressure from oil, tariffs and AI investment.

Some on Wall Street expect the Fed to wait this week. Goldman Sachs sees a skip in October followed by a hike in December, which would leave Zandi’s argument as much a test for the December meeting as for this one.

[2026-10-27 00:22 UTC] PBOC is expected to set the USD/CNY reference rate at 6.6731 – Reuters estimate

Tags: CNY, pboc, CNH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

The downward revision to August makes the September print more hawkish than the headline suggests: services prices are accelerating, not just holding. Japan’s services producer price index rose 3.7% year on year in September, in line with expectations and faster than a downwardly revised 3.5% gain in August, Bank of Japan data showed on Tuesday. The pick-up points to mounting service-sector price pressure ahead of a BoJ meeting this week that is expected to leave rates on hold while signalling further tightening ahead.

The revision changes the picture. August’s reading was initially reported at 3.7%, which had suggested September would merely match the prior month. With August now at 3.5%, the September figure marks an acceleration rather than a steady pace, a more hawkish read for policymakers watching whether inflation is becoming entrenched in the domestic economy.

The index tracks the prices companies charge each other for services such as transport, communications, finance, real estate, advertising and equipment leasing. The BoJ watches it closely for evidence of how far firms are passing rising labour costs on to service prices, a central part of its case that wage growth and inflation are reinforcing each other. A faster pace suggests that pass-through is gathering momentum rather than fading.

The data contrast with the goods side, where producer price inflation slowed to 6.8% in September from 7.6% in August as fuel subsidies eased some raw-material costs. Services prices rising while goods inflation slows suggests domestic, labour-driven pressure is taking over from imported costs as the main driver.

The BoJ concludes a two-day meeting on Friday and is expected to keep its policy rate at 1.25%, after raising it in September, while signalling it is ready to continue lifting borrowing costs as price pressures build. Most economists expect the next hike in December. The key focus is whether the bank’s updated Outlook Report effectively declares that underlying inflation has reached its 2% target, a step it has never taken since adopting the goal in 2013.

Today’s acceleration strengthens the case for that declaration and makes it harder for the board members who opposed September’s hike to argue for easier policy.

Summary

Weaker loan demand and tighter credit conditions pose fresh questions over rate hike expectations for December in particular

The ECB just released its latest bank lending survey and it's not exactly painting a particularly encouraging picture for the euro area economy. Of note, banks are tightening their lending standards further while demand for loans is falling across the board, largely due to the backdrop of higher interest rates continuing to weigh on borrowing activity.

According to the October survey, which covers developments in Q3, the main takeaways are:

Corporate lending:A net 9% of banks reported tighter credit standards, exceeding the 5% previously expected

Consumer credit:A net 14% of banks reported tighter lending standards, while housing loan standards were unchanged

Corporate loan demand:Fell by a net 6%, reversing a 3% increase in Q2

Housing loan demand:Slumped by a net 27%, compared with a 15% decline in Q2

In looking to Q4, banks also expected further tightening in lending standards and even weaker household loan demand. So, that's not a good sign of things to come.

In any case, the numbers reinforce the message that higher interest rates are already doing their job in slowing the economy. Softer borrowing and tighter credit conditions should eventually weigh on investment, consumption and broader economic activity as a whole.

And with banks also needing to navigate through geopolitical uncertainty and worsening risk perceptions, there is little reason to expect credit conditions to improve in the near-term.

As such, that continues to leave the ECB with a relatively uncomfortable balancing act to manage.

Traders are currently pricing in roughly 90% odds of no change in November, so the immediate decision looks fairly straightforward. But December is another matter, with markets assigning around 70% odds of a 25 bps rate hike.

So, today's lending survey only serves to give the central bank another reason to tread carefully before committing to further tightening. Of course, the inflation outlook remains the bigger consideration especially with energy prices still posing upside risks. But if incoming data continues to soften, then markets may have to reconsider just how much additional tightening the ECB can realistically deliver.

For now, December still looks to be a plausible option for another rate hike. However, the case to tighten further beyond that may be less clear amid ongoing fiscal worries and a slowdown in economic momentum.
