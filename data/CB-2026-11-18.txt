Summary

PBOC CNY reference rate setting for the trading session ahead.

The People’s Bank of China is due to set the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT (2115 US Eastern time), a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the renminbi (yuan) is allowed to trade within a prescribed band around a central reference rate, or midpoint, set each trading day by the PBOC. The current trading band permits the currency to move plus or minus 2% from the official midpoint during onshore trading hours.

Each morning, the PBOC determines the midpoint based on a range of inputs. These include the previous day’s closing price, movements in major currencies, particularly the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic economic considerations such as capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The midpoint is not a purely mechanical calculation, allowing policymakers discretion to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is announced, onshore USD/CNY is free to trade within the allowable band. If market pressures push the yuan toward either edge of that range, the central bank may step in to smooth volatility. Intervention can take the form of direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance through state-owned banks.

As a result, the daily fixing is often interpreted as a policy signal rather than just a technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected CNY midpoint is typically read as a sign the PBOC is leaning against depreciation pressure, while a weaker fixing for the CNY can indicate tolerance for a softer currency, often in response to dollar strength or domestic economic headwinds.

In periods of heightened global volatility, such as shifts in US rate expectations, trade tensions or capital flow pressures, the fixing takes on added significance. For investors, it provides insight into Beijing’s currency priorities, balancing competitiveness, capital stability and financial market confidence.

Six of nine RBA board members backed November’s hike to 4.85%, judging the cash rate “not clearly sufficient” to address upside inflation risks.

Most Reserve Bank of Australia board members judged that the cash rate was “not clearly sufficient” to bring inflation under control when they voted to raise it to 4.85% this month, according to minutes of the November meeting released on Tuesday. A three-member minority warned the economy might be close to a materially sharper downturn.

The minutes lay bare a board divided over the balance of risks. The six members who backed the 25 basis point increase, the fifth hike of the year, said forward-looking inflation pressures had risen and the risks to the outlook remained to the upside. They judged that the danger of leaving policy insufficiently restrictive, and allowing inflation expectations to drift higher, outweighed the risk of tightening too much.

The majority pointed to three signs that domestic capacity pressures were playing a bigger role than previously thought: strong inflation in market services, persistent growth in unit labour costs, and broad-based cost pressures reported by business contacts. Staff have revised up their estimate of the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation, which implies the labour market was tighter in 2026 than they had assessed. Members also noted that the neutral interest rate had likely risen, which makes any given cash rate less restrictive than before.

The three dissenters preferred to hold at 4.60%. They argued it was prudent to wait and assess the 100 basis points of tightening already delivered this year. They placed more weight on downside risks to growth, citing a half-percentage-point rise in unemployment over six months, the housing downturn and fragile sentiment toward investment in data centres.

The Middle East conflict featured heavily. Members noted that crude and refined fuel prices had risen 20% to 25% since August. The forecasts assume oil prices fall by around US$30 a barrel over two years, an assumption that requires some resolution of the conflict and leaves significant upside risk if prices stay high. Staff expect headline inflation to jump in October as last year’s energy subsidies drop out.

The board offered no explicit guidance, saying only that it will remain attentive to the data and do what is necessary to return inflation to target. Earlier on Tuesday, Governor Michele Bullock said inflation risks are still skewed to the upside. Wage data on Wednesday and October jobs figures on Thursday are the next tests.

Summary

Former BOJ chief Kuroda says rates may need to rise above neutral if inflation worsens, citing Iran war cost pressures. He called Takaichi’s food tax cut inflationary and Japan’s Q3 contraction probably temporary, ahead of an expected December hike.

For Japanese rates and the yen, a call for above-neutral rates from the architect of Japan’s easing era adds weight to the hawkish tail of BOJ pricing. Most economists see a terminal rate near 1.75%, and talk of 2.5% now has a high-profile backer. Kuroda no longer sets policy, so the direct effect is limited. Former Bank of Japan Governor Haruhiko Kuroda says interest rates may need to rise above the neutral level if inflation pressures intensify, according to an interview reported by Bloomberg via The Japan Times. It is a striking message from the architect of a decade of ultra-loose policy.

Kuroda said the outlook depends entirely on inflation. If price pressures become more serious, he said, rate hikes would naturally have to speed up and could take the policy rate beyond neutral, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. He pointed to the cost pressures Japan faces from the US war with Iran, given the country’s heavy reliance on imported oil, raw materials and food.

His comments come as investors focus on how far the BOJ’s tightening cycle can run. The central bank is widely expected to raise rates next month. The median economist forecast sees the policy rate peaking at 1.75%, roughly two more hikes, though some see it reaching as high as 2.5%. About two weeks ago, the BOJ board under Governor Kazuo Ueda judged underlying inflation to be consistent with its target for the first time since the goal was set in 2013. Kuroda called that a welcome development but said the bank’s job is not done.

He also took aim at fiscal policy. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi plans to cut the sales tax on food for two years from April, a package expected to add around ¥10 trillion, or roughly $63 billion, to the economy. With unemployment at just 2.4% and the economy running at capacity, Kuroda said the tax cut would push up prices rather than growth. That would leave the BOJ no choice but to keep raising rates.

Data released on Monday showed Japan’s economy contracted in the July to September quarter, with consumer spending hit partly by unusually bad weather. Kuroda played down the figures, describing the weakness as probably temporary.

The interview also offered a rare look back. Kuroda served two terms and is the longest-serving governor in the BOJ’s history. In a book published last month, he revealed that when the bank introduced negative rates of minus 0.1% in 2016, he believed it would be difficult to cut further, despite the bank’s repeated pledges to do so if needed. Financial institutions, he said, may not have been able to absorb deeper negative rates.

[2026-11-17 02:14 UTC] NZ 1-year inflation expectations jump to 2.9%, but the 2-year anchor barely moves

Summary

New Zealand one-year inflation expectations jumped to 2.92% from 2.60% in the RBNZ survey, but the closely watched two-year gauge edged up only to 2.36%. Respondents see the OCR at 3.05% by year-end, above today’s 2.75%, before the 9 December MPS.

For the RBNZ, the survey is mixed rather than alarming. The stable two-year reading lets policymakers argue that expectations remain anchored. The one-year jump and the higher OCR expectations show forecasters now firmly expect tightening. That supports pricing for a hike at the 9 December Monetary Policy Statement, and any pushback from the RBNZ would surprise. For the New Zealand dollar, near 0.55, higher rate expectations give some support, though the move in the two-year gauge is too small to be a strong driver on its own. The slump in house price expectations is a reminder that tighter policy is already biting. That may temper how far the RBNZ is willing to go.

New Zealand forecasters are bracing for higher inflation next year but still trust the RBNZ to bring it back over two years. They also expect it to raise rates to get there.

One-year inflation expectations rose to 2.92% from 2.60%, and two-year expectations edged up to 2.36% from 2.34%.

Five-year and 10-year expectations fell, to 2.17% and 2.13%.

Respondents expect the OCR at 3.05% by the end of December, above the current 2.75%, and at 3.40% in a year.

Annual CPI inflation was 3.7% in the September quarter, still above the RBNZ’s 1% to 3% target band.

One-year-ahead unemployment expectations held at 5.24%, while wage expectations rose to 2.90%.

One-year house price inflation expectations fell to just 0.51%.

New Zealand businesses and professional forecasters lifted their near-term inflation expectations sharply in the fourth quarter, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand’s latest Survey of Expectations showed on Tuesday. But the two-year measure the RBNZ watches most closely barely moved.

One-year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 2.92% from 2.60%, a jump of 32 basis points. Two-year-ahead expectations edged up to 2.36% from 2.34%. Longer-term measures eased: five-year expectations fell to 2.17% from 2.31%, and the 10-year gauge slipped to 2.13% from 2.20%.

Why the two-year number matters

Policymakers focus on the two-year horizon because it roughly matches how long it takes for changes in interest rates to work through the economy. If businesses expect inflation to stay high over that period, they are more likely to build it into wages and prices, which makes inflation harder to bring down. A two-year reading close to the middle of the RBNZ’s 1% to 3% target band suggests expectations remain anchored, even though the short-term outlook has worsened.

The jump in the one-year measure probably reflects recent experience. Annual CPI inflation was 3.7% in the September quarter, down from 4.1% in the June quarter but still above the target range. The survey went into the field on 23 October, the day after those figures were released.

Rate expectations move higher

Respondents also expect higher interest rates. The mean expectation for the official cash rate at the end of the December quarter was 3.05%, above the current 2.75%. That implies an expected hike at or before the RBNZ’s next Monetary Policy Statement on 9 December. One-year-ahead OCR expectations rose to 3.40% from 3.21%.

The rest of the survey was mixed. One-year-ahead unemployment expectations held at 5.24%, one-year wage inflation expectations rose to 2.90%, and GDP growth expectations improved. House price expectations fell sharply, with house price inflation one year ahead now seen at just 0.51%.

What to watch

The firmer one-year reading combined with a stable two-year anchor gives the RBNZ room to argue that expectations are under control. But the rate expectations in the survey show that respondents believe more tightening is coming. The 9 December statement will show whether the central bank agrees.

Summary

Goldman Sachs Asset Management expects Fed hikes in both December and January, then a prolonged pause, after US core CPI rose 0.3% in October for a third firm print. Broad price gains make it harder for the Fed to look through the strong data.

A January hike is a step beyond what the market has firmly priced, so Goldman’s call leans against any rally at the front end. The two-year yield’s jump to near 4.86% shows how quickly expectations can move. For the dollar, a longer tightening cycle supports the policy divergence already weighing on currencies such as the yen and the Aussie. Goldman Sachs Asset Management expects the Federal Reserve to keep raising interest rates beyond December, after a third consecutive strong reading on US core inflation. It forecasts back-to-back hikes in December and January, followed by a prolonged pause.

Core consumer prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3% in October. That is an annualized pace of about 3.6% and was above market expectations. Goldman said the run of three firm prints suggests the softer readings over the summer were temporary rather than the start of a sustained slowdown in inflation. Its charts show the three-month annualized rate of core inflation climbing back to around 3.7%, and the year-on-year measure has edged higher in recent months.

What concerns the asset manager most is the breadth of the increase. Strength showed up across several categories at once. One was so-called supercore inflation, which tracks services prices excluding housing and is closely watched by the Fed as a gauge of wage-driven pressures. Housing and core goods also rose. Goldman argued that such widespread gains make it harder for policymakers to dismiss the data as noise, particularly while the labor market remains resilient.

Markets have moved in the same direction. The two-year Treasury yield, the maturity most sensitive to Fed expectations, had jumped to around 4.86% as of November 11, according to Goldman’s figures, a sharp recovery from a dip in October.

Goldman’s base case is for consecutive hikes in December and January, followed by an extended hold. It cautioned, however, that further increases could follow if inflation proves sticky. For portfolios, the firm said it remains cautious on US interest rates, citing resilient growth and inflation momentum that reinforce the Fed’s hawkish bias.

The view adds to a growing debate over how far the tightening cycle has to run. Investors will look to the next round of inflation and labor market data, and to Fed officials’ comments ahead of the December 8-9 meeting, for signs of whether a January move is gaining support inside the central bank.
